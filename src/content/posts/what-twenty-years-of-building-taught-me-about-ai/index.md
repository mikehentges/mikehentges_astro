I didn't come to AI from a research lab or a startup incubator. I came to it from two decades inside enterprise technology organizations — first as CTO of a Fortune 500 systems integrator, then as IT Director at a manufacturer. The path wasn't obvious, but in hindsight, it was the best possible preparation.

When you spend twenty years building complex systems under real constraints — budget pressure, legacy dependencies, organizational politics, and the unforgiving reality of production data — you develop a particular kind of pattern recognition. You learn to see the shape of a problem before you see the solution. You learn which architectural decisions survive their first encounter with reality and which ones collapse. You learn the difference between a technology that changes how work gets done and a technology that just changes how people talk about work.

That pattern recognition is the most valuable thing I bring to AI conversations today.

## The patterns that transfer

Every wave of enterprise technology follows a similar arc. Early adopters get real value because they have the fundamentals to absorb the new capability. The broader market gets mostly confusion because they're trying to use the new technology to solve problems the old technology wasn't causing.

I watched this happen with cloud migration. The organizations that got the most from the cloud were the ones that already understood their workloads, their data patterns, and their operational requirements. They used the cloud to do what they were already doing, better and more flexibly. The organizations that struggled were the ones hoping the cloud would somehow fix their unclear architecture or their undocumented dependencies. It didn't. It just moved those problems to a more expensive address.

AI is the same story, one layer up. The teams getting extraordinary results from AI-assisted development already had clear codebases, well-defined interfaces, and engineers who could evaluate generated output. The teams struggling are the ones hoping AI will compensate for the organizational clarity they never built.

## What manufacturing taught me

After twenty years in enterprise services, I took a hard left turn into manufacturing. ERP systems. MRP runs. Shop floor data. Supply chain logistics. It was a completely different world, and it taught me something I couldn't have learned in consulting: what "production-grade" means when the stakes are physical.

In manufacturing, the inventory numbers are either right or they're wrong. The production line either runs or it doesn't. There's no "we'll fix it in the next sprint." That experience stripped away every remaining instinct to over-engineer, over-promise, or mistake a demo for a solution.

When I evaluate AI tools and AI-generated output today, I apply that same standard. Does this work in production? Not in a demo. Not in a controlled environment. In production, with real data, real users, and real consequences. The answer is often "not yet" — and that's fine. "Not yet" is honest. "Not yet" is something you can plan around. The dangerous answer is "probably," because "probably" ships and then fails at 2 AM on a Saturday.

## The practitioner's advantage

There's a version of AI advisory work that operates at the strategy layer — frameworks, roadmaps, maturity models. That work has its place, but it's not what I do. I work at the intersection of strategy and implementation, where the interesting question isn't "should we use AI?" but "how do we use AI without breaking what already works?"

That question requires someone who has built production systems and understands what breaks. Not theoretically — actually. Someone who has been paged at midnight because a system they built failed, and who carries that experience into every design decision. AI doesn't change the fundamentals of building reliable systems. It changes the speed at which you can build them — which means it also changes the speed at which you can break them.

The best AI adoption strategies I've seen look less like technology initiatives and more like organizational discipline. They start with clear requirements. They invest in architecture that can absorb AI-generated code without becoming fragile. They keep experienced people in the loop — not as bureaucratic gatekeepers, but as quality amplifiers.

## What I've learned

Twenty years of building taught me that every technology wave rewards the same things: clarity, fundamentals, and experienced judgment. AI is the most powerful wave I've seen, but it doesn't change that equation. It raises the stakes.

The organizations that will get the most from AI are the ones that were already good at building. AI makes them faster, more capable, and more ambitious. The organizations that were struggling before AI will struggle with AI too — just with newer tools and higher expectations.

If you're a technology leader trying to figure out what AI changes for your organization, my advice is simple: start with what you already know about building things that work. The principles haven't changed. The tools have gotten remarkably better. And the gap between teams that build well and teams that don't is about to get a lot wider.
